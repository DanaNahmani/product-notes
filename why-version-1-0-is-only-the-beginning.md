# Why version 1.0 is only the beginning

Releasing version 1.0 can feel like the finish line.

In practice, it is usually the point where a different kind of work begins.

Before release, most decisions are based on design, testing, assumptions and beta feedback.

After release, the product starts meeting real users in situations I cannot fully predict.

That changes things.

## Version 1.0 proves the idea

The first public version has one main job: turn an idea into a real product.

It establishes the core experience.

It shows what the product is, how it works and which problems it is trying to solve.

But it does not need to contain every idea that could possibly belong in the product.

Trying to build everything before the first release can make a product larger without necessarily making it better.

## Real use creates better questions

Once a product is available publicly, new questions appear.

Which features are actually useful?

Where do users hesitate?

Which parts feel natural?

What is missing?

What looked important during development but rarely matters in practice?

Those questions are difficult to answer completely before a product exists in the real world.

## Updates are not only about adding features

A new version does not have to be bigger than the previous one.

Sometimes an update adds a new capability.

Other times it improves navigation, wording, responsiveness, consistency or a small interaction that makes the entire product feel better.

Removing friction can be just as valuable as adding functionality.

## The product develops its own direction

As versions accumulate, the product begins to reveal what it should become.

Some early ideas grow into important parts of the experience.

Others become less relevant.

New priorities appear through testing, feedback and continued use.

That is why I try not to treat the original plan as something that must remain unchanged.

The product should be able to evolve.

## Shipping creates momentum

A released product can be observed, tested and improved.

An unreleased product can only be imagined.

Once version 1.0 is released, the next version is often already moving through development and QA.

Shipping creates a clear milestone, but the work continues — refining, testing and preparing what comes next.

For me, that is why shipping is not the end of development.

It is the beginning of the product’s next stage.
